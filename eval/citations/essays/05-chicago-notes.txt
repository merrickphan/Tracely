Movable Type and the Speed of the Reformation

The printing press did not create religious dissent, but it changed how fast dissent could travel.¹ Manuscripts circulated at the speed of a horse and the cost of a scribe; a printed pamphlet circulated at the speed of trade.

Luther's Ninety-Five Theses were reprinted across the German-speaking lands within weeks of being posted.² Contemporary accounts describe presses in Nuremberg and Leipzig running his tracts continuously through 1518 (ibid., 47). By 1520 he was the most published author in Europe, and the gap between him and the second was not close.³

The economics matter more than the technology. A press paid for itself only where enough literate buyers lived within a few days' travel, which is why early printing clustered in trading cities rather than university towns (op. cit.).

Historians disagree about how much the press caused rather than accelerated. Eisenstein's account treats print as a genuine agent of change, while Johns argues that the fixity she attributes to print was itself constructed slowly and unevenly.⁴ The disagreement is less about the evidence than about what counts as a cause.

What is not in dispute is the timeline. A movement that would have taken a generation to reach the edges of Europe reached them in under five years, and every institution that tried to suppress it was working against a distribution system it did not control.
