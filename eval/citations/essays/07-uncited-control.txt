Why My Town Should Rebuild the Public Pool

The old pool closed in 2019, and nothing has replaced it. Attendance had been falling for years before that (down 4% since 2015), but the closure was decided on maintenance cost rather than on use.

The council's own figures make the case badly. The facilities report lists the annual operating deficit (Table 3) and explains how it was calculated (Chapter 11), but neither section says what the pool was worth to the families who used it. A deficit is not the same as a waste.

Swimming is the only sport in town that does not require a car to reach. Every other facility sits past the ring road, which is fine if you are sixteen and someone will drive you, and useless if you are eleven and nobody will. My brother stopped swimming entirely the year it shut (he was nine).

The obvious objection is money. Rebuilding would cost more than repairing, and the town has already raised rates twice since the closure (up from 2019). But the repair estimate assumed the same building, and the same building is why the deficit existed.

There is a version of this proposal that is affordable. A smaller pool, open fewer months, run in partnership with the school district, would cost roughly a third of the figure in the report. State law limits what the town can raise directly (Proposition 13), so partnership is not optional, it is the only route available.

The council has already agreed the principle once, in the resolution it called ("A Pool for Every Neighbourhood"), and then funded none of it. My mother asked the chair about it at the June meeting and he said "we'll see".

The question is not whether a pool pays for itself. Nothing the town builds pays for itself. The question is what a town is for, and a town where the only free thing to do in July is sit in a parking lot has answered that question badly.
