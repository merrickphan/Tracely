Antibiotic Resistance in Hospital-Acquired Infections

Resistance to last-line antibiotics has risen faster than the rate at which new classes reach clinical use [1]. The pipeline problem is structural: a drug reserved for resistant infections is by design prescribed rarely, so it earns little and is developed by almost nobody [2,3].

Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales are the clearest example. Mortality in bloodstream infections caused by these organisms is roughly double that of susceptible strains [4]. Hospital surveillance in twelve European countries recorded a threefold increase in isolates between 2007 and 2015 [5-7].

Stewardship programmes reduce inappropriate prescribing, but the measured effect on resistance rates themselves is smaller than the effect on prescribing volume [8]. This distinction is frequently collapsed in policy summaries, which report the easier number.

Rapid diagnostics may matter more than stewardship in the long run. Identifying an organism in two hours rather than two days removes the clinical reason for broad-spectrum empirical therapy in the first place [9]. Trials of these platforms have shown shorter time to targeted therapy without worse outcomes, though none were powered to detect a resistance effect [10].

The most likely near-term outcome is that resistance continues to rise while the response remains fragmented across national systems. Coordinated surveillance would at least make the trajectory visible before it becomes irreversible.
