The Cost of Free Parking

Donald Shoup argues that free curb parking is not free at all, only paid for by someone other than the driver (Shoup 45). The cost is folded into rent, into the price of groceries, and into the shape of the city itself.

Minimum parking requirements force developers to build spaces whether or not anyone wants them. In many American cities the required ratio was copied between municipalities with no local study behind it (Shoup 22-24). One survey of ordinances found that fewer than a tenth cited any empirical basis at all (Willson 34).

The result is visible from any downtown parking structure. Land that could hold housing holds cars instead, and the housing that does get built costs more because each unit carries a garage it may never use (Manville and Shoup 279). Cities that removed their minimums saw small infill projects become viable again almost immediately (Gabbe and Pierce 112).

Critics respond that removing minimums simply pushes cars onto neighbouring streets. That is a real effect, and it is why priced curb parking has to arrive alongside the repeal rather than years after it (Shoup 501-503).

There is a counterargument worth taking seriously. In cities with weak transit, a household without a car is a household without a job, and parking policy cannot fix a transit problem on its own.

Parking minimums should be repealed and replaced with market-priced curb parking. The policy is unusual in being both cheap to enact and immediately felt.
