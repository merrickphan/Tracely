Johannes Gutenberg introduced movable-type printing to Europe around 1450, and within fifty years printing presses were operating in more than two hundred European cities. The conventional story treats this as the moment literacy and scientific progress became inevitable, but the causal chain is considerably messier than that.

Movable type had already existed in Korea and China for centuries before Gutenberg, which complicates any account that treats the technology itself as the decisive variable. What distinguished the European case was the alphabet's small character set, an existing paper industry, and a merchant class with money to spend on books. Cities that adopted printing early experienced faster economic growth in the following century, though disentangling that from the fact that wealthier cities could afford presses in the first place is genuinely difficult.

The press also spread material that its champions would not have chosen. The Protestant Reformation depended on cheap pamphlets, and so did an enormous volume of witch-hunting literature. Any technology that lowers the cost of distributing ideas lowers it for bad ideas too, which is a lesson the present century has had ample opportunity to relearn and has mostly declined to.
