My uncle finished school at sixteen in Stuttgart and spent the next three years splitting his week between a vocational school and a machine shop that paid him while he learned. He is fifty now and has never been out of work for longer than a month. Whenever anyone in my family talks about college debt somebody brings him up.

Germany's dual system places roughly half of each cohort into apprenticeships that combine classroom instruction with paid work at a company, and the qualification at the end is recognised nationally rather than by the one employer who trained you.

Youth unemployment in Germany has stayed below the European Union average for most of the last two decades.

That is the part people point at.

Nancy Hoffman's Schooling in the Workplace argues that the American high school treats vocational training as what happens to students who could not manage the academic track, and that this attitude, rather than the funding, is the difference that matters.

I do not know whether it would work here. The counselors at my school have one pathway and it ends at a four-year university, and nobody I have asked can tell me what the other one would even look like.
