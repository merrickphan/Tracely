Mary Shelley's Frankenstein is usually read as a warning about science, but the novel is far more interested in what Victor does after the creature opens its eyes than in the act of creation itself. The moment of animation takes up barely a paragraph. Abandonment takes up the rest of the book.

Victor's first response to his own success is to leave the room. Shelley gives him no reflection, no attempt at communication, nothing but flight, and so the creature's entire education happens in his absence, assembled from a stolen bag of books and a family he watches through a wall. When the creature finally speaks, he speaks in the register of the books he found, which is why his eloquence startles readers who came expecting a monster.

The novel was published anonymously in 1818, and the revised 1831 edition carries a new preface in which Shelley describes the book's origin during a stay at the Villa Diodati. Read against that preface, the famous phrase about the "workshop of filthy creation" sounds less like laboratory horror than like secrecy. The workshop is filthy because it is hidden.

What makes the creature tragic is not that he was made. It is that he was made and then left, and that every cruelty he commits afterward is an answer to that first one.
