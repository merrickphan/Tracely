Almost every serious plan for reducing global poverty assumes electricity, and almost none of them explains where it comes from. Roughly half the population of Sub-Saharan Africa still lacks access to electricity, a share that has barely improved in two decades even as the region's population has grown. The result is that the absolute number of people without power has stayed nearly flat while the percentage slowly falls, which is the kind of statistic that lets everyone claim progress.

The standard response is off-grid solar, and at small scale it has genuinely worked. Household solar systems can now power lighting, phone charging, and a radio for under a hundred dollars, and adoption across East Africa has been rapid. But a solar home system delivers on the order of tens of watts, while a refrigerator, a welding shop, or an irrigation pump needs kilowatts. Access measured as "any electricity at all" counts a household that can charge a phone as connected.

Critics of grid investment point out that centralized generation in low-income countries has a poor record: transmission and distribution losses in some national grids exceed twenty percent of all electricity generated, and state utilities often cannot collect enough revenue to maintain what they have already built. That is a fair description of the last thirty years. It is not an argument that distributed generation solves industrial demand, which is the demand that actually correlates with leaving poverty.

The honest position is that energy access has been defined down to whatever the cheapest intervention can deliver. Counting connections is easy and counting kilowatt-hours is not, so the metric that gets reported is the one that flatters the programs. Until access is measured by what it lets a household or a business actually do, the numbers will keep improving while the problem stays where it is.
