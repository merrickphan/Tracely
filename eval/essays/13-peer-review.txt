Peer review gets described as science's immune system, and the last few years have been a good argument that it works. They have also been a good argument that it works slowly, and that the slow part is the part the public sees.

In May 2020 a study of roughly ninety-six thousand hospital patients reported that those given hydroxychloroquine died at rates about a third higher than those who were not, and within days the World Health Organization suspended the hydroxychloroquine arm of its largest trial.

The paper was retracted less than two weeks after it appeared, once three of its four authors said they could not verify the patient data the company behind it had supplied.

That is the system working. It is also the system working after the headlines, after the trial pauses, and after several million people had already read the number.

Retractions are rare enough that most readers will never knowingly encounter one, since fewer than one published paper in a thousand is ever withdrawn.

So the honest summary is that hydroxychloroquine raised mortality by roughly a third and the correction was mostly a bookkeeping exercise about who could take credit for showing it. Science got there. It just got there second, and nobody has figured out how to make the retraction travel as far as the finding did.
